Opacity,
index.

Opacity, in one sentence: a system is opaque with respect to a secret if an observer, watching only what it's allowed to see, can never become certain that the secret occurred. Above: two runs that differ only in whether the secret event happens, but agree on every observable event, so the observer, seeing only the teal dots, can't tell which run actually took place.
● What opacity is

Opacity is a confidentiality property for systems whose behavior is only partly visible to an outside observer. A system is opaque with respect to a chosen secret if, no matter what that observer sees, it can never become certain the secret occurred. Equivalently: for every run that reveals the secret, there must exist some other run, indistinguishable to the observer, that doesn't.

The notion originates in the discrete-event-systems and labeled-transition-system literature of the mid-2000s, and has since been formalized, verified, and enforced across a wide range of system models: plain finite automata, Petri nets (bounded and unbounded), pushdown automata, timed automata, timed process algebra, and more. The core question stays the same everywhere it goes, but the answer, and the machinery needed to get it, changes considerably depending on what kind of system is being asked about.

Pages in this Index
01

Classical Systems

The classical setting, and where the field started: finite automata, labeled transition systems, and bounded Petri nets, generally nondeterministic, with a single observer. The largest and most developed page in the index.

 02

Timed Systems

Systems where the observer also reads off when events happen, not just their order, typically via timed automata and real-valued clocks. Timing becomes a side channel in its own right.

 03

Stochastic Systems

Systems with randomness: probabilistic automata, hidden Markov models, and Markov decision processes. Strict yes/no opacity gives way to quantifying disclosure risk.

 04

Distributed Systems

Systems observed by more than one site. The underlying system stays finite-state; what changes is the observation architecture.

 05

Infinite-State Systems

What happens once the finite-state assumption is dropped: pushdown automata, unbounded Petri nets. A much smaller, younger corner of the literature, in part because many natural questions here turn out to be undecidable.

 06

Continuous-State Systems

Cyber-physical and control systems whose state evolves continuously. Exact indistinguishability gives way to a bounded, metric notion of closeness.

 07

Weighted Automata

Systems whose transitions carry algebraic weights, typically over the max-plus (tropical) semiring. Information can leak through the weight itself.

 08

Related Problems

Neighboring notions that keep surfacing while researching opacity: detectability, diagnosability, non-interference, and a few others. This page makes no attempt to be exhaustive about them.
